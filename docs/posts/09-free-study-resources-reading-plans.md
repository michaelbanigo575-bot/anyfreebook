You have been told a book is free. You clicked through, saw a cover image and the word "Read", and either hit a paywall three paragraphs in or got forty pages of a two-hundred-page book and a "buy" button. You downloaded it anyway, and it now sits in a folder called PDFs you have not opened since. You have done this eleven times.

The problem is not that free books are hard to find. It is that "free" and "available" are the same thing, and they are not. Libraries are full of records: metadata about books you cannot download, catalogue entries for titles whose only copy sits in a reading room in Boston, and links that resolve to a lending queue rather than a file.

## What the guide is

The guide is a working map of the open shelf — what each source actually holds, how large it is, what format it uses, what it is good for, and where it stops — followed by the procedures for turning any of it into a plan you finish.

### Six checks to tell a real download from a catalogue record

This is the section that saves the most time. The signals are concrete:

| Signal | Real download | Catalogue record |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| Link target | Ends in .pdf/.epub/.zip/.mp3 | Ends in /item/, /record/, /search? |
| File size shown | Yes | No |
| Licence shown | CC BY, CC BY-SA, Public Domain | "All rights reserved" or nothing |
| Works offline | Yes | No, needs the site |
| Text searchable in your own tools | Yes | No |
| Lasts if the site changes | Yes, you have the file | No |

Then the three-minute habit, applied to anything you plan to build a term on:

- **Look for the file, not the "Read" button.**
- **Check what the download actually is.** A "PDF" that is one page saying "purchase access" is a teaser. Read the last page of the introduction. If the last chapter is missing, it is a sample.
- **Check whether an account or an institution is required.** Institutional access is still paywalled if you are outside the institution.
- **Look for the licence.** No licence means all rights reserved.
- **Look for a second copy.** An OpenStax PDF plus a LibreTexts version plus an Internet Archive scan is a real open book.
- **Use the bibliographic record to confirm the edition.** Edition mismatches cost more marks than missing books.

The licence point deserves its own note. A Creative Commons licence controls reuse, not price. **CC BY** lets you copy, adapt and redistribute with attribution. **CC BY-SA** asks you to keep the licence on adaptations. **CC BY-NC** forbids commercial use. **CC BY-ND** forbids adaptations. Those differences matter the moment you want to print a chapter, translate it, put it in a course pack, or republish it.

### Building a reading list that gets finished

Most free reading lists fail at week three, and the reason is arithmetic, not laziness. The guide's fix is the **three-list system**: exactly one current book on your desk, a queue of the next three, and an unlimited, unranked, guilt-free someday list.

The mix matters too, and it is not a link dump:

- **One authoritative book on the subject — 40%.** Gives you the vocabulary and the shape of the field.
- **Three to five open textbooks and course notes — 30%.** Structured, tested, and they come with exercises.
- **Two or three classic or public-domain works — 20%.** Context, and the pleasure of reading something older.
- **Two or three recent papers — 10%.** Keeps you current and sharpens the other ninety per cent.

The plan also has to survive a bad week, so each of the three twelve-week plans has a 30-minute version:

| If you have | Do this | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30 minutes a day | One chapter of the anchor book, 40 pages | Continuity beats volume |
| 30 minutes a week | One page of notes on where you got to, plus one question | Prevents restart friction |
| 30 minutes a month | One podcast episode on the subject | Keeps the thread alive |
| 30 minutes and no signal | A Gutenberg plain-text file, offline | Works with no connection |
| 30 minutes and a tired brain | One classic, or one audiobook | Low-effort entry keeps the chain |

Above those sit three twelve-week plans: **Plan A**, the steady reader, three sessions a week at about 40 pages; **Plan B**, the exam candidate, six sessions a week, targeted; **Plan C**, the researcher, writing fortnightly at around 10 pages a week.

## The search behind it

ANYFREEBOOK searches these libraries live — Project Gutenberg, Open Library, the Internet Archive, Google Books, PubMed Central, DOAJ, LibriVox, OpenAlex, Wikisource, Europeana, Zenodo and the Open Textbook Library — and marks every result as a **real download**, a **borrow-only copy**, or a **catalogue record**. That labelling is the product: a search engine cannot tell you the link is a lending queue, and neither can most library catalogues, so a student ends up downloading forty samples and calls it research.

Where the shelf is thin, the guide says so, and points to a real alternative. Its common mistakes are about over-collection:

- **Treating a search result as a book.** Try one title end to end before trusting it.
- **Downloading a sample and calling it a book.** Read the last page of the introduction first.

- **Building a list of fifty books.** Cut it to 40 per cent, then pick one as the current book.
