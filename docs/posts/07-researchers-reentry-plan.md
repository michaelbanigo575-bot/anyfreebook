You have a topic, a supervisor's name, and a folder of PDFs you have not opened. The literature review is supposed to be next, and the literature is a wall: three hundred papers, no obvious entry point, and a growing suspicion that everyone else in the department already knows how to get through it. You have read about research methods. You have not read any research.

Or maybe you are further along and the problem has changed shape. You finished the data collection, the draft exists, and the desk rejection arrived with the sentence "not suitable for this journal", which tells you almost nothing and lands like a verdict on your ability. Either way, the obstacle is rarely the subject. It is that the work is unbounded and there is no manual telling you the order of operations.

Research is unusual among the things people return to, because there is no lecture timetable to hand you the shape of a week. The reading list is a literature nobody sequenced for you, and the deadline is a submission date two years away that feels both distant and immovable.

## What the guide is

The researcher's restart is organised as a re-entry, like the other guides on the site: it assumes the hardest part is the restart rather than the research. It covers finding a direction, reading a literature that is too big, writing a first paper, and surviving a rejection.

### Finding a direction

The starting move is to stop trying to be interesting and start trying to be specific. A workable research question in the first year is usually a narrowing, not a discovery: a population, a period, a practice, a contradiction in the literature that somebody noticed and nobody followed up. The guide treats the direction stage as a search with a defined exit, because "read more and something will emerge" is not a method, it is a hope with a reading list attached.

### Reading a literature that is too big

This is the part most people get wrong, and the part where the guide is most concrete. It works from a **six-pass reading procedure** rather than a skim-and-highlight:

1. **Pass one, the abstracts.** Ten to fifteen abstracts, written in your own words, one line each. You are mapping the territory, not reading.
2. **Pass two, the introductions.** Why does this paper exist? What gap does it claim? Most of the field's disagreements are visible here and invisible in the methods.
3. **Pass three, the claims.** What exactly is being asserted, and with what strength? "Suggests", "shows" and "proves" are different words and are used carelessly.
4. **Pass four, the method.** Sample, data, analysis. This is the pass that tells you whether the claim is supportable, and it is the one most people skip.
5. **Pass five, the evidence.** The tables and figures. If the numbers do not say what the prose says, that is a finding, and it may be your finding.
6. **Pass six, the limitations.** What did the authors admit, and what did they not? The unstated limitations are where your project lives.

The companion idea is the **evaluation questions**: for each paper, what is the method, what is the sample, what is the population, what is the comparison, and can the claim be transferred to your setting? Five questions, asked of every paper, produce a comparison table instead of a pile of summaries.

| Pass | What you read | What you write down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Abstract only | One line per paper: what it claims |
| 2 | Introduction | The gap it says it fills |
| 3 | Claims | The claim, and how strongly it is stated |
| 4 | Method | Sample, data, analysis |
| 5 | Tables and figures | Whether the numbers say what the prose says |
| 6 | Limitations | What was admitted, and what was not |

Search comes before reading, and the guide is blunt about the **preprint problem**: preprints give you speed and currency, but they are unreviewed. Use them to find the argument, not to cite the result.

### Writing a first paper, and the rejection

The writing section works in a fixed order — framing, method, results, discussion, introduction — with the introduction deliberately written last, because it is the only section that depends on knowing what you actually found. That single reordering removes a common disaster: a paper that promises more than its results support.

For rejection, the guide's position is that a desk rejection is a routing decision, not a verdict, and the useful response is to read the editor's line as a specific question rather than as a verdict about you. The practical steps are short:

- **Extract the reason.** Scope, fit, evidence, or a missing piece they want. Write it down in one sentence.
- **Decide which kind it is.** A fit problem needs a different journal. An evidence problem needs more work.
- **Change exactly one thing.** Not three. One change, then resubmit to a list rather than to the same journal immediately.
- **Keep a rejection log.** Journal, date, editor's reason, what you changed. After four rejections the pattern is usually obvious and it is usually not the thing you assumed.
- **Deposit the draft anyway.** A public preprint of a rejected draft is a real output and a real citation, not a consolation prize.

## The open literature, for free

A researcher can read a great deal without a single institutional login, and this is the part that changes how a project starts:

- **PubMed Central** — free full text in biomedicine and the life sciences.
- **DOAJ** — peer-reviewed open-access journals and their metadata.
- **Zenodo** — datasets, preprints and software deposited with a DOI.
- **OpenAlex** — the scholarly graph: citation counts, related works, author and institution records.
- **Wikisource and Europeana** — primary sources that most people only meet as scans.

Then the book side:

- **Project Gutenberg** — the classic theoretical texts most fields argue with.
- **Open Library and the Internet Archive** — borrowing anything still in copyright.
- **Google Books** — checking editions, dates and pagination.
- **LibriVox** — the audio version of a public-domain monograph you would otherwise not read.
- **The Open Textbook Library** — the methods and statistics texts your department would charge you for.
