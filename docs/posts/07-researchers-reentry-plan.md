You have a broad area, a supervisor, and a deadline somewhere in the distance. You do not have a research question. You have a folder of PDFs you opened once, and every time you sit down to read, the literature gets larger rather than smaller. Six months in, you realise you have been consuming and not producing, and the annual review is closer than it was.

Most new researchers do not fail because they cannot read. They fail because they read for six months and then discover that reading is not writing. A literature does not get smaller when you consume it. It becomes a room you are standing in, with walls you cannot see past.

The trouble is that almost all advice on this is either encouragement or a reading list. The useful thing is a procedure: what to write down, in what order, and what "done" looks like each month.

## What the guide is

It is a working system rather than encouragement, and it is written as a checklist you run weekly rather than a book you read once. It covers the first six months of a project, a triage method for a literature you cannot read, a note format that turns reading into an argument, a twelve-week plan that ends with a submitted paper, and an appendix that takes you from a blank page to a submitted draft in a fortnight.

### The first six months are for narrowing, not producing

The standard advice to spend a year reading is bad advice for anyone with a funding review or a supervisor who asks what you did this month. The first six months have one job: turn a broad area into a question small enough to answer in a year.

Before opening a database, the guide asks for twenty minutes writing half a page about what you currently believe about the field. What is the central disagreement? What do your colleagues argue about at coffee? Once you hold a position, you can read for it, and reading for a position is roughly three times faster than reading in order to be informed.

Three artefacts should exist at the end of month six:

- **A one-page scoping memo.** The question in one sentence, why it matters in three sentences, and what already exists that you have checked.
- **A triage list of forty to sixty papers.** Every paper you looked at, each with a one-line verdict: core, useful, background, or irrelevant.
- **A two-page proposal.** The question, the method, the data, the timeline, and the two risks you think will kill it.

And there is a six-month map with hours per week attached, from twenty in month one to thirty in month six.

### The three-pass method

This is the spine of the guide, and it has a table with a time budget per paper:

| Pass | Time per paper | What you read | What you write |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: Survey | 15 to 20 minutes | Abstract, intro, figures, conclusion | One sentence: what did this change? |
| 2: Argument | 45 to 60 minutes | Full text, methods and results included | Three claims, marked agree, dispute, or unclear |
| 3: Deep | 2 to 3 hours | Everything, plus supplement and data | A note per claim, with page numbers |

The rule that cuts most noise: only papers with at least one *dispute* are worth a pass three. Agreeing papers are for citations, not for the argument. And the honest arithmetic that follows — aim to finish pass three on five to eight papers per month, not thirty. Twenty-five full readings in a year is already serious work.

### One note per claim, not per paper

A paper usually contains three to seven claims that matter to you. The guide's fix is to stop taking notes about papers and start taking notes about claims: the claim in your own words, the source, the exact location, the evidence type, your verdict.

Then there is the **evidence ledger** — one table of every claim your paper will make, one row each, with the best source found so far and a status of supported, weak, missing, or contested. The procedure is blunt: if more than three claims are marked missing, you are not writing yet. Go and find the evidence.

The guide also has a **disputes file** — every time a paper contradicts your working position, write both claims side by side and say which you believe and why. That file gives you the shape of your contribution, and it supplies the last paragraph of your introduction, which is where weak papers usually die in review.

### Writing, and surviving a rejection

IMRaD with word budgets, so the paper is a finite object: a 200-to-250-word abstract, a 600-to-900-word introduction, 700 to 1,200 words of methods. That totals 3,700 to 6,000 words for a first paper, which is normal. Outline first, prose second, and draft each section in a separate file with a filename and a word target.

The rejection section is the one I would hand to a colleague. It gives a **two-week response procedure**: wait three days before replying to anyone, then separate the reviews into three lists — genuinely fatal, fixable, and misunderstanding or preference. Most reviewers' comments fall into the third list, and conflating the three is what makes people give up.

The steps, in order:

- **Wait three days.** Read the reviews once, write down everything that stung, and reply to nobody.
- **Separate the three lists.** Fatal, fixable, and misunderstanding-or-preference. Most comments sit in the third.
- **Read the reviews again as a stranger.** If two independent reviewers mention the same gap, it is a gap.
- **Decide the journal separately from your mood.** If the top journal rejects you, submit to the second on your list within a week, with the referee reports attached.
- **Revise before you resubmit.** Three weeks minimum. Revising with the reports in front of you is a rewrite, not a patch.
- **Answer every point.** Quote each comment, answer it in one to three sentences, and do not argue in the letter.

The closing line is the point of the whole guide: a rejected paper with a written revision plan is worth more than an accepted paper you learned nothing from.

## The open literature, for free

The guide has a section on open access that is practical rather than idealistic, including a five-step route to a specific paper you cannot afford: find the DOI, try the resolver, try Unpaywall, search the repository layer, then ask the author. Every paywalled paper has a legal route.

ANYFREEBOOK searches the open libraries live — Project Gutenberg, Open Library, the Internet Archive, Google Books, PubMed Central, DOAJ, LibriVox, OpenAlex, Wikisource, Europeana, Zenodo and the Open Textbook Library — and marks each result as a **real download**, a **borrow-only copy**, or a **catalogue record**. For a researcher that third label matters most: a link that says "free" and gives you a lending queue or a forty-page sample will not put a citation in your paper.

The three tiers, in plain terms:

- **Real download** — a free, legally readable copy of that exact title, resolved from the source's own metadata.
- **Borrow-only copy** — a real Internet Archive copy that the Archive's own record says is loan-only. There is no file to fetch.
- **Catalogue record** — a record or a search-results page. The book exists; the copy does not.
