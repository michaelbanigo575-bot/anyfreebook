## Back to school encouragement strategies: for the student who has been away

This is the guide for anyone whose last attempt ended badly. The memory of a failed term is more vivid than the evidence that you can finish one, and that is the real obstacle — not the material, not the money, not the family.

The guide's premise is that the returning student who succeeds is not the one who wants it more. It is the one who built a system that still works on the worst week. So it starts by removing friction, not by generating motivation: a seven-day reset before term (paperwork and money first, because late applications are the most common reason a return gets expensive; then the physical backlog; then a timetable built around a real week rather than an ideal one), and then one habit at a time.

Two ideas from it are worth repeating here. The **floor, the standard and the stretch**: you design the routine around the floor — the version you can still do on a bad week — and treat the standard as the target. And the **60/30/10 split** of a study week: 60% new material, 30% consolidation through retrieval and rewriting notes in your own words, 10% admin batched into one session. Returning students invert that, spending 60% on notes because notes feel productive and 10% on practice because practice feels slow. Marks come from practice.

## The first 30 days at university: for the student who has never done this

Nobody tells you how a week is supposed to work. Your lecturer talks about an essay with a reading week in between. Your flatmate has a colour-coded planner that looks like a decoration. Somebody in the seminar asks a question that assumes you have read four books already.

The guide's first week has five things that must work by Friday: a timetable block, a deadline register, the reading list downloaded while the links still work, three names in your phone, and a place to work. Everything else can wait.

Then the ratio that decides the term: **for every hour of lecture, plan two hours of independent work**. And the notes: the Cornell page, with a cue column and a summary strip written after the session, which is a forty-eight-hour test — if you cannot write the summary, you did not understand it, and now you know what to re-read.

## A reading and writing plan for new researchers

Most new researchers do not fail because they cannot read. They fail because they read for six months and then discover that reading is not writing.

The guide's answer is a three-pass method with a time budget per paper: a fifteen-minute survey that produces one sentence, a forty-five-minute argument pass that produces three claims marked agree, dispute or unclear, and a deep pass only for papers where something is disputed. Agreeing papers are for citations, not for the argument.

The second idea is the **evidence ledger** — one row for every claim your paper will make, with the best source found so far and a status of supported, weak, missing or contested. If more than three claims are missing, you are not writing yet. It is blunt, and it is the difference between reading and producing.

## A first week of teaching that does not humiliate anyone

For the lecturer coming back after a break, the uncomfortable finding is that the commonest student complaint is not difficulty with the content. It is not knowing what is expected.

The guide is a fourteen-day plan before teaching and a four-week plan after, with minute-by-minute session plans. Day 14 audits the course against last year. Day 9 publishes the deadline map. Day 10 designs an ungraded diagnostic of eight questions. The rule for week one is specific: **no new required reading**, because students arrive with a backlog they did not choose.

And the part most courses never design for: the first session is a baseline and a first win, an eight-question diagnostic done alone and then in pairs, and a worked example from the messy original to the clean answer.

## Free textbooks, open courseware, and how to tell a real download from a catalogue record

The reference guide is the one people come back to. It lists the twelve open libraries with what each one actually holds and where it stops, then gives six checks that separate a file you can keep from a link that only looks free:

| Signal | A real download | A catalogue record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Link target | Ends in .pdf, .epub, .zip, .mp3 | Ends in /item/, /record/, /search? |
| File size shown | Yes | No |
| Licence shown | CC BY, CC BY-SA, public domain | "All rights reserved", or nothing |
| Works offline | Yes | No, it needs the site |
| Lasts if the site changes | Yes, you have the file | No |

The licence point is the one that catches people. A Creative Commons licence controls **reuse, not price**. CC BY lets you copy, adapt and redistribute with attribution. CC BY-SA asks you to keep the licence on adaptations. CC BY-NC forbids commercial use. CC BY-ND forbids adaptations. Those differences matter the moment you want to print a chapter, translate it, put it in a course pack, or republish it — which is exactly what happens in a school.
