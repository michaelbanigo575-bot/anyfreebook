Nobody tells you how a week is supposed to work. Your lecturer talks about an essay with a reading week between now and it. Your flatmate has a colour-coded planner that looks like a decoration. Someone in your seminar asks a question that clearly assumes you have read four books already. Meanwhile you have attended three lectures, understood perhaps half of each, and are quietly convinced that everyone else got a different set of instructions.

The first week of university is not about learning. It is about finding out where everything is and who to ask. Students who treat week one as a study sprint arrive at week two exhausted and behind. Students who treat it as a logistics project arrive with a timetable, three names in their phone and a clear idea of what the course expects.

## What the guide covers

The guide runs the first month day by day, but the useful part is the reasoning underneath it.

### Week one: five things that must work

By the end of the first week you should have:

1. **A working timetable block.** Two or three fixed slots per week, protected as if they were lectures. Even 90 minutes each.
2. **A deadline register.** One page: every assessed deadline, exam date and reading week date, with the week number next to each. Update it every Sunday.
3. **The reading list, downloaded.** Every item, saved now while the database still works and the links are live. Books marked "available in the library" often are not, and the reserve shelf has a three-day loan limit.
4. **Three names.** One lecturer or module leader, one administrative contact, one classmate who seems to know how things work.
5. **A place to work.** On campus, find the room you will actually use. At home, clear the kitchen table.

On day one the guide says the most important thing you do is find out where the reading list lives and how you submit work. Two questions, asked at the induction desk, the lecturer, or a second-year. Write the answers down.

### The lecture-to-review ratio

The rough, tested rule: **for every hour of lecture, plan two hours of independent work.** One hour preparing the next lecture, one hour reviewing the last. A module with 10 lecture hours a week means 20 hours of independent work, which sounds brutal until you realise reading is not the same as sitting at a desk staring at a wall.

| Situation | Lecture hours | Independent hours | Weekly total | Realistic? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reading-heavy humanities module | 10 | 25-30 | 35-40 | Yes, if you read on paper and take notes actively |
| STEM module with problem sets | 12 | 24-30 | 36-42 | Yes, if problem sets are done weekly, not in a rush |
| Assessment-heavy final-year module | 8 | 30-40 | 38-48 | Yes, but term-time pressure rises |
| Group project module | 6 | 20-25 | 26-31 | Yes, plus meeting time you cannot control |
| Part-time or employed | 5 | 12-15 | 17-20 | Yes, cut lecture replay, keep reading |

The ratio is not about precision. Students under-budget independent work, then blame themselves at the end. Budget it up front.

### The three ways a week can go

Every week goes one of three ways, and the difference is almost always whether you read before the session that needed it. A **type A week** is prepared: you read first, the lecture confirms what you half-know, the tutorial feels like a conversation. A **type B week** is reactive: you did not read, you understand perhaps 40 per cent in the room, and you spend Sunday reconstructing the lecture from slides. A **type C week** is detached: you did not read, the lecture went over your head, you avoided the tutorial, and you have decided this week will be reclaimed later. It will not be — it costs 2 hours of avoidance now, then 15 hours of catch-up in week seven plus guilt.

The rule is unglamorous: read before the session, always, even if the reading is only twenty minutes.

### Notes worth having in March

The guide compares five note-taking methods honestly, then does something better: it shows the **Cornell page** concretely. A narrow right-hand cue column with questions you would actually ask, a wide left column with notes in your own words, and a two-to-three-sentence summary strip written after the lecture. That summary strip is a 48-hour-old test — if you cannot write it, you did not understand the session, and now you know what to re-read.

There is also a **weekly note-cleaning routine**: twenty-five minutes, Sunday, every week, not optional and not "when I have time".

And a set of reminders about where to keep notes:

- **One place per module.** Notes scattered across six apps are notes you cannot find in week nine.
- **Searchable filenames.** A file called `lecture-03-ch4.docx` costs nothing to rename now and everything later.
- **Backups you do not have to think about.** Cloud sync, not a USB stick in a drawer.
- **A format you can read on a bad day.** Text and paper beat a PDF of slides when you are tired and on a train.

### The timetable, the catch-up plan, the money

The timetable section gives you a template and then a 30-minute procedure for building your real one: list every fixed commitment first, mark your peak hours, then place study into the gaps. Saturday and Sunday carry the flex, so if you are behind, Sunday's catch-up blocks absorb the gap without destroying the week.

When you fall behind, the guide is specific about it in five steps — measure the gap honestly, triage by category, apply a plan matched to how far behind you are, use the five-hour catch-up day, and prevent the next gap. It also lists the ten mistakes that cost students whole terms, and the three that matter most:

- **Treating the first week as a study sprint.** Week one is for building the machine, not for running it.
- **Choosing a timetable you would like rather than one you can keep.** A timetable that fits your life is worth more than an ambitious one you abandon.
- **Rereading notes instead of retrieving.** Close the book and write what you remember.

There is also a two-week revision plan built on active recall rather than rereading, plus sections on money, admin, housing, and the adjustments first-generation, mature and international students need.

## The free libraries your course is built on

A university course assumes you own its books. You often do not. The guide has a section on finding the free version of each required text and a table of what each source is actually good for:

- **The Open Textbook Library** — comparing whole-module texts, with reviews and adoption history.
- **OpenStax** — peer-reviewed textbooks for first-year maths, science and economics.
- **LibreTexts** — collaborative notes, best for filling one specific gap quickly.
- **Project Gutenberg** — public-domain literature and language texts, in EPUB and plain text.
- **Open Library and the Internet Archive** — borrowing the in-copyright edition of your course text.
- **PubMed Central and DOAJ** — current articles in life sciences and peer-reviewed open journals.
- **MIT OpenCourseWare and OpenLearn** — full course materials if you want to study a course you are not enrolled in.
