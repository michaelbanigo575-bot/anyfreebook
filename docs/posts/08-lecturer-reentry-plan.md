Your slides feel behind. The reading list feels like a novel you wrote in another life. And the first email to the class — a subject line you have rewritten three times — feels like a first day. Then remember that you have had a break and they have not. Between the last session and the first one, your students had three weeks of unstructured reading, an exam period that cost some of them more sleep than it should have, and possibly a summer job, a hospital admission, a visa appointment or a family crisis.

The single most common finding in student feedback about returning to a course is not difficulty with the content. It is not knowing what is expected of them.

Three things are true of almost every returning class, and the guide treats all three as design problems rather than personality problems. They have lost the plot, not the ability: they can still reason, but they cannot reconstruct the vocabulary and shape of the course unaided. They are running on depleted attention, and most adults hold focused attention on a single complex narrative for fifteen to twenty-five minutes before performance drops. And they are performing for you: the quietness in the room is caution, not indifference.

## What the guide is

The guide is a fourteen-day plan before teaching, then a four-week plan after, with concrete timings, marks, session plans and checklists. It is written for lecturers, tutors, teaching fellows and graduate teaching assistants.

### What the students are feeling, and what to do about it

| What the student is feeling | How it appears in the room | The move that helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unsure what is expected | Silent first week, no questions, everything waits for a deadline | State the next three deadlines out loud, in writing, in week one |
| Behind on reading | Blank faces at the start of the second session, no homework done | Set one small, completable task per week, not five |
| Anxious about being wrong | No eye contact, no questions, head down | Model your own error explicitly, once, early |
| Lonely | Arrive and leave alone, no study partner, no office hours visit | Build in a pair that repeats, not a one-off mingle |
| Oversubscribed with life | Late, absent at 09:00, coursework piled on other modules | Publish a deadline map; flag the heaviest week |
| Convinced they cannot do the subject | Asks permission to skip, avoids the hardest exercise | Put one achievable win in week one |

### Three questions to ask before term

Do not guess at their state. Ask, cheaply, and use the answers. First: **what was the last thing you remember from this course?** A free-text box, three lines, no marks — it tells you what survived the break. Second: **which weeks of the course feel heaviest right now?** A multiple-choice grid over the term weeks, so you know where to spread the load. Third: **what would make the first three sessions useful rather than stressful?** Free text, read every answer before the first class meets.

Put these in a five-minute anonymous form. If your institution has no survey tool, use a shared document or a one-question-per-line form; the format does not matter, the anonymity does. Ten to twenty responses will change your plan. Send it at least five days before the first session, and again after week three.

The guide also insists on a small set of commitments before term, because the re-entry problem is mostly a scaffolding problem:

- **Rebuild the map before the detail.** One slide, one diagram, one page: where this course came from, where it is going, and what sits between here and the final assessment.
- **Remove the first hurdle.** Do not start with a definition nobody has met. Start with a question they can answer.
- **Name the break out loud.** Say that you have been away, that the material has moved, and that the first sessions are for getting back up to speed.

### The fourteen-day plan

Each day is 60 to 90 minutes of actual work. Day 14 is an audit against last year to find what has to be rebuilt from scratch. Day 13 is a one-paragraph course promise — what a student can do by week 12 that they cannot do now — reduced to a single sentence used in week one. Day 12 rebuilds the session skeleton for weeks one to four, minute by minute. Day 11 chooses the three week-one readings and checks each is readable in under 40 minutes. Day 10 designs the low-stakes diagnostic: eight questions, ten minutes, ungraded, no notes. Day 9 publishes the deadline map.

The guide's advice on scope is the part experienced lecturers recognise: returning lecturers tend to over-change. Sort every element into three buckets and be ruthless about the third:

- **Keep.** Anything that worked, that students use, and that you can explain in one sentence. In most courses this is 60 to 70 per cent.
- **Change.** Anything that depends on a dead assumption: a pre-requisite nobody teaches any more, an assessment format students hated, a schedule that assumed a week of preparation you now know does not exist.
- **Retire.** Anything kept from habit or guilt. A course with fifteen readings instead of twenty-five is a better course.

The rule for week one is specific: **no new required reading**. Students arrive with a reading backlog they did not choose. Start the term with material you know cold, then add the demanding reading from week three, once the class has settled.

### The five-session first week

The session plan is minute-by-minute, which is what makes it usable. Session one opens with the course promise and the deadline map, then an ungraded diagnostic of eight questions done individually and then in pairs — a baseline plus a first win — then a worked example of one real problem, from the messy original to the clean answer. After the break there is a mini-lecture that rebuilds the map of the course with three ideas and three anchors, a think-pair-share on the anchor question with four answers taken, and a close that says what happens next and where to ask. Sessions two and three use a retrieval warm-up of three questions from the previous session, small-group tasks with roles printed on the sheet, and a marked sample answer compared against two student attempts. Session four ends with the week-one debrief. Session five is an in-class quiz marked by students against the answer key, then a clinic where students bring one problem and work it in pairs.

## Building the course on free material

There is a section on the open teaching material your course can be built on, with an honest note on what free does and does not mean:

- **OpenStax** — peer-reviewed textbooks in maths, science, business and economics.
- **MIT OpenCourseWare and OpenLearn** — full course materials: lecture notes, problem sets, reading lists.
- **LibreTexts** — collaborative notes for filling one gap in a reading list.
- **The Open Textbook Library** — comparing whole-module texts, with reviews and adoption information.
- **Project Gutenberg** — primary-source literature in plain text and EPUB.
- **Open Library and the Internet Archive** — borrowing anything still in copyright.
- **DOAJ and PubMed Central** — current peer-reviewed research.
- **Zenodo** — material you deposit yourself, with a DOI.

ANYFREEBOOK searches those libraries live, along with Google Books, Wikisource, Europeana, OpenAlex and LibriVox, and marks each result as a **real download**, a **borrow-only copy** or a **catalogue record**. For teaching this matters twice over: it keeps you from putting a dead link in a reading list, and it keeps you from telling a student that a book is free when it is only borrowable.
